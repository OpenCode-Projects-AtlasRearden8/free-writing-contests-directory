Rights and exclusivity, in plain English

You can sell "first publication" of a story and still own it forever — unless fine print says otherwise.

Friend-to-friend summary: rights are just permissions. When a magazine says "we want first rights", they mean "we would like to be the first to publish this." They are not asking you to hand over your story. The dangerous requests are the ones with the words all, perpetual, irrevocable, or copyright assignment in them.

The words you'll actually see

FASIP
"First (serial/analog/…) rights" — the right to publish your piece first. After their issue comes out, the right is used up and the story is yours to publish again. This is the standard ask of reputable magazines.
First publication rights
Same idea as FASIP, said plainly: the first public release of the piece.
One-time / one-shot rights
Permission to print once, in one place. Used by many anthologies. Reprinting again usually needs to go back to you.
Reprint / second rights
Permission to publish a piece that has already appeared somewhere. Reputable markets say explicitly whether they take reprints (most pro SFF markets don't, or pay less for them).
Non-exclusive rights
"You may publish elsewhere simultaneously or afterwards." The friendliest arrangement. Availability to republish stays yours.
Exclusivity
Only they may publish the piece during a stated period. Common and reasonable if it's 3–12 months and dated. Unreasonable if it's "forever" or tied to the life of the story rather than a period.
All rights
The single worst phrase for a writer: you're assigning everything — print, audio, film, translation, derivative works — usually forever, often for little pay. If you see this with no real money attached, close the tab.
Copyright transfer / work-for-hire
Sometimes called "assignment". Legally, the piece becomes theirs; for a contest entry, that's effectively buying artwork from you at the price of entry (which was free). Almost never worth it.
Archive / perpetual posting
Online magazines often keep their issues posted indefinitely with the piece identified. That is normal and fine if the archive clause says the piece is attributed to you and the rights stay bounded.
Option
A first chance to consider a follow-up deal (e.g. reprint, translation) before others. Only off-putting when the option is binding or undated.

Exclusivity, in plain English

Exclusivity is a time-boxed no-competing clause for the piece you sent them, and it has two flavors:

  • Hard exclusivity — the piece cannot appear anywhere else while the window runs. This is what magazines are usually buying when they pay professional rates for first publication.
  • Soft exclusivity — you and any other market that already accepted the piece are fine, but the piece shouldn't appear newly anywhere else until they've run it. Common for anthologies coordinating publication dates.

The repairable problem is when exclusivity has no end. Rules pages that say "entrant grants exclusive rights until the organizer's use is complete" are asking you to hand over an unbounded period. A fair ask looks like:

Example of a reasonable clause (approximately): "The publisher acquires first publication rights for 6 months from the release date; all other rights return to the author, who is free to resell the piece in the interim." Ask politely — you can negotiate before accepting an offer, though not after.

What to ask for when in doubt

  1. A written contract that says which rights are granted, not just "we reserve the right to use your work".
  2. An end date to any exclusivity window.
  3. Your name spelled right. A byline is real value; a contract granting posting-without-credit is different from one promising attribution.
  4. What payment (if any) accompanies the grant. Professional markets pay per word (recent common ranges run roughly 8–10 cents per word in the SF/F pro tier; always check the current stated rate); token payments are usually a few dollars to a few dozen, flat.
  5. Reapproval for edits. Copy-edits that fix spelling are standard. Substantive changes to a story's meaning should come back to you.
  6. Termination notice. "If we cease operations, rights revert to the author" is a line worth requesting from smaller markets.

The AI-era addition: entry agreements sometimes add "we reserve the right to create derivative works, or use the entry to train models." That is a rights grant; if it isn't a deal you would accept for money, it isn't one you should accept for free.

Certain terms that show up within contracts, as a mini-glossary

Subsidiary rights
Spin-off sales like translations, film, or audio. Publishers list what they control and what they pass to you; anthology contracts usually spell out that translation and derivative rights stay with the author.
Rights reversal
Formal notification that the publisher's window is up. You often don't need it, but it's a clean record to have when others ask "has this been published?"
Simultaneous submissions
Sending one piece to several markets at once. Some markets allow it (with a note), others don't; honoring the stated rule is basic professional courtesy.
Multiple submissions
Sending several pieces to the same market. Most have a per-piece cap per window.
Withdrawal
Telling the market the piece is no longer available (usually because another one accepted it). Most submission portals have a button; otherwise, a short polite email.

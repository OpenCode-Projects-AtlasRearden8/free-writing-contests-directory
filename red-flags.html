Red flags: fee traps and rights grabs

How to spot the contests and "anthologies" that take your work — or your money — and give little back.

The one-sentence rule: a legitimate free contest or magazine is free at every step, asks for clearly defined rights, and pays or publishes winners without asking anyone for money back. If any part of that chain reverses — you pay, they keep rights, they ask winners to buy things — walk away.

Fee-trap red flags

  • An "entry fee" that arrives after a "free" headline. Free-opportunity posts that end with "$25 to submit" are fee contests, full stop.
  • "Reading fee." Prestigious literary magazines get enough submissions that they don't need to charge readers; most real fee-free markets run on subscriptions, donations, or university support.
  • "Membership required to enter." A contest bundled so you must purchase a membership is a fee contest with extra steps.
  • "Processing fee for finalists" or "publication fee for winners." Never legitimate. Winners should receive money, not send it.
  • "Your prize includes attending our conference / publishing package." If the "prize" is a discount on paid services, the prize is your wallet.
  • Everyone gets a certificate. Vanity operations ("your poem is a finalist!") monetize entry by selling printed anthologies, plaques, and certificates back to participants.
  • "Optional" paid add-ons that stack. $5 critiques, $10 chosen-legacy highlights, $20 faster review — the base fee was never the point.
  • High-pressure countdowns. Hourly "spots closing" emails are marketing, not contest administration.

Vanity publishing, plainly: some presses (such as the many brands operated by Author Solutions / Author House-style companies, widely reported by watchdogs over the years) earn money by selling publishing packages to the author, not by selling books to readers. Symptom: enthusiasm up front, "work with our team" language, fees discussed later, no standard royalty statement demanded by you, no real editorial gate.

Rights-grab red flags

  • "By submitting, work becomes the property of the organizer." Full stop — that is a copyright transfer for little or no money. Genuine free contests use a narrow license over winning or published entries only.
  • Licenses with no end date and no scope. "Entrants grant a perpetual, irrevocable, worldwide, transferable license to use entrant's work without compensation" is designed for reposting your story anywhere, forever, for free.
  • "Sole rights" or "first rights forever." Publication rights should be scoped: first publication only, usually 3–12 months exclusivity, then back to you (see the Rights page).
  • Blanket clauses in the rules that apply to all entrants, not just winners. A rule "we may publish any submission in any manner" means you could lose your story without a byline or a dime even if you lose.
  • Anthology contracts demanding "copyright" instead of "publication rights." Anthologies normally buy one-time anthology rights. A publisher demanding copyright on an unpaid anthology is assembling a rights portfolio, not publishing a book.
  • Contributors must buy their own copies. Reputable anthologies, even token-paying ones, send contributors a copy.
  • Vague translation/derivative clauses. Watch for wording like "adapt at our discretion" or "edit for length and content" with no final-approval step (reasonable magazines and anthologies show you substantive edits).
  • AI-training clauses buried in entrant agreements. Some photo/essay contests now claim the right to use all entries to train models. If you see "machine learning" or "derivative works" language, that is the deal.

Other warning signs

  • No named judges or editors. Rule of thumb: real contests list real names because their reputation is the product.
  • No past winners. A contest with several years running but no winner archive is not advertising its proudest moment. Ask yourself why.
  • The "official" site is actually a reseller. Found the contest through an emailed "you've been selected" or a third-party listing? Confirm the contest itself exists by typing the organizer's web address yourself rather than following links in messages.
  • Winner claims that don't match the payout. "Top-ten publication in a printed book" where that book is sold back to entrants is not a "status award". Legit anthologies are bought by readers, not by their contributors.
  • No contact information or a mismatched domain. A "national" contest run from a webmail address is a warning sign. Domain name should match the named organization.
  • Gushing generic congratulations. "Our respected panel has selected your remarkable piece for recognition" emails sent to thousands of entrants are a specialization of the certificate-and-print-run model above.

Green flags (things that make you safer)

  • Named judges with published work. Especially judges whose inclusion makes sense.
  • Winner archives with years of names. That archive is the contest's track record; a few minutes of clicking tells you whether it is real.
  • Rates and rights written in the first paragraph, not the last one. Professional markets put payment and exclusivity up front.
  • A response-time and query window. "We reply in 4–8 weeks; query after 10" is industry-standard clarity.
  • Fee listed as $0 or nowhere at all. Paying markets that are free to submit are the default in that part of the industry that pays professional rates.
  • Narrow scope. "First publication rights for 6 months, nonexclusive thereafter" beats "all rights, forever" every time, and narrow is usually a sign the people running it have been paid too.

Education only — not legal advice. The examples above describe widely reported industry patterns, not accusations about specific current organizations.
